# Kongzi learning-science basis

Status: CONFIRMED evidence review with INFERRED product applications  
Reviewed: 2026-07-26

## Purpose

This document defines which learning methods Kongzi may use, how strongly the
evidence supports them, and where they do not generalize. It is a product
evidence source, not a claim that one technique works equally well for every
person, domain, or task.

## Executive conclusion

There is no credible universal “best learning method.” The most defensible
default is a coordinated loop:

1. diagnose prerequisites and define observable capability;
2. use concise instruction or worked examples for new material;
3. require active retrieval or application;
4. give specific corrective feedback;
5. revisit the material after a delay;
6. mix related problem types when discrimination matters;
7. monitor errors and re-plan.

The method should change with the knowledge type and learner evidence. Facts,
concepts, procedures, judgment, motor skills, and creative work need different
proof.

## Evidence grading used here

- **High**: replicated evidence, a major review/meta-analysis, and broad
  relevance to Kongzi's use.
- **Moderate**: credible experimental or review evidence with meaningful domain
  boundaries.
- **Conditional**: useful in defined contexts but not a general learning rule.
- **Do not use as a personalization basis**: insufficient evidence for the
  claimed instructional adaptation.

This is a product evidence label, not a formal GRADE assessment.

## 1. Retrieval practice / testing effect

Evidence: **High**

Roediger and Karpicke found that repeated retrieval produced better delayed
retention than repeated study, even when repeated study produced higher
confidence. Butler later reported advantages for transfer to new inferential
questions, not only repetition of the same question.

Product application:

- ask before showing;
- prefer free recall and production over recognition alone;
- capture learner confidence before feedback;
- retest after a delay;
- vary questions when transfer is the goal;
- pair retrieval with correction so errors do not persist.

Boundary:

- testing is a learning event, but a poorly aligned question can train the
  wrong target;
- one successful immediate retrieval does not demonstrate durable mastery;
- novices may need an initial example before meaningful retrieval is possible.

Primary sources:

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-enhanced
  learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x>
- Butler, A. C. (2010). *Repeated testing produces superior transfer of
  learning relative to repeated studying*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019902>
- Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). *Retrieval practice produces
  more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327>

## 2. Spacing / distributed practice

Evidence: **High**

Cepeda and colleagues' quantitative synthesis found a robust distributed
practice effect and showed that useful spacing depends on the desired retention
interval. Their later long-duration study found that the best gap grows as the
final test moves farther into the future.

Product application:

- schedule retrieval according to retention horizon, not a universal fixed
  interval;
- expand intervals after successful delayed retrieval;
- shorten them after error, low confidence, or overdue review;
- make the schedule inspectable and editable.

Boundary:

- a scheduler is a heuristic, not evidence that material was learned;
- spacing without retrieval or meaningful re-engagement can become repeated
  exposure;
- optimal gaps vary with material, learner, and final-test timing.

Primary sources:

- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D.
  (2006). *Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and
  quantitative synthesis*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354>
- Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H.
  (2008). *Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal
  retention*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x>

## 3. Interleaving and varied practice

Evidence: **Moderate; domain-sensitive**

Rohrer and Taylor found large delayed benefits when mathematics practice was
spaced and problem types were mixed instead of blocked. Interleaving is
especially relevant when the learner must identify which strategy applies.

Product application:

- after basic instruction, mix confusable concepts or problem types;
- ask the learner to select the method, not merely execute a named method;
- use comparison and contrast questions;
- record discrimination errors separately from execution errors.

Boundary:

- interleaving is not random disorder;
- a novice who has never seen a valid example may first need blocked,
  supported practice;
- benefits depend on category structure and the target task.

Primary source:

- Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). *The shuffling of mathematics
  problems improves learning*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8>

## 4. Self-explanation and the useful core of the Feynman technique

Evidence: **Moderate to high for self-explanation; the named “Feynman
technique” itself is not a universal research-backed protocol**

Chi and colleagues found that stronger learners generated more explanations
linking example steps to principles and monitored their own understanding more
accurately. This supports asking the learner to explain why a step works, how a
claim follows, and where their understanding breaks.

Product application:

- ask the learner to explain in their own words without the source open;
- require examples, non-examples, mechanisms, and boundary conditions;
- compare the explanation to sources and a rubric;
- identify the first unsupported or vague step;
- re-explain after correction.

How Kongzi treats the Feynman technique:

- “Explain simply” is useful but insufficient.
- Simplicity is not proof of correctness.
- The effective product loop is retrieval → self-explanation → source/rubric
  comparison → gap diagnosis → corrective study → re-explanation.
- It is well suited to conceptual understanding and communication.
- It cannot replace repeated procedural practice, perceptual discrimination,
  motor practice, or real-world performance evidence.

Primary source:

- Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R.
  (1989). *Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in
  learning to solve problems*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1>

## 5. Worked examples, cognitive load, and fading

Evidence: **Moderate to high for novices in structured problem domains**

Sweller and Cooper's algebra experiments supported worked examples as a way to
develop schemas without forcing novices to spend most effort on unguided search.

Product application:

- start a novice with an annotated worked example;
- ask for self-explanation of steps;
- use completion problems that remove some steps;
- fade support until the learner solves independently;
- switch earlier to independent practice when the baseline shows expertise.

Boundary:

- worked examples can become passive reading;
- examples must match the target problem structure;
- excessive guidance can become redundant for knowledgeable learners;
- open-ended judgment and creative work require cases, critique, and
  production—not only worked solutions.

Primary source:

- Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). *The use of worked examples as
  a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3>

## 6. Mastery learning

Evidence: **High for examination performance, with cadence costs**

Kulik, Kulik, and Bangert-Drowns synthesized 108 controlled evaluations and
reported positive effects of mastery-learning programs, with larger effects for
weaker students. They also found increased time on task and lower completion in
some self-paced college implementations.

Product application:

- define explicit evidence required for each prerequisite node;
- allow different time, practice, and corrective paths;
- do not advance through critical prerequisites solely because a date arrived;
- add cadence, recovery sessions, and follow-up to avoid self-paced stalling.

Boundary:

- mastery thresholds must align with the target capability;
- the approach may increase time required;
- “mastered” is not permanent; delayed evidence can decay;
- completion and exam performance do not cover every real-world capability.

Primary source:

- Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A., & Bangert-Drowns, R. L. (1990).
  *Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: A meta-analysis*.
  <https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265>

## 7. Feedback and error correction

Evidence: **High that feedback matters; effect depends on type and delivery**

Hattie and Timperley's review emphasizes that feedback effects can be positive
or negative and depend on what information the feedback supplies. A later
meta-analysis found a medium-to-high overall effect with substantial
variability.

Product application:

- answer: Where am I going? How am I going? What should I do next?
- focus feedback on task, process, strategy, and self-regulation;
- show the specific mismatch and request a re-attempt;
- store misconception type and whether correction transferred;
- avoid substituting generic praise for information.

Boundary:

- feedback can overload or demotivate when mistimed or excessively controlling;
- giving the full solution too early removes retrieval and diagnosis;
- correctness feedback alone may not repair the underlying model.

Sources:

- Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). *The power of feedback*.
  <https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487>
- Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). *The power of
  feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research*.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/>

## 8. Active learning

Evidence: **High in undergraduate STEM; broader implementation still needs
domain judgment**

Freeman and colleagues' meta-analysis of 225 studies found improved examination
performance and lower failure under active learning compared with traditional
lecturing in undergraduate STEM.

Product application:

- no session completes from reading alone;
- use questions, problems, comparisons, predictions, critiques, and projects;
- treat the learner's output as diagnostic evidence;
- keep explanations short enough to preserve practice time.

Boundary:

- “active” describes cognitive engagement, not merely clicking or talking;
- the evidence base here is strongest in undergraduate STEM;
- activity without alignment to a learning objective is not automatically
  beneficial.

Primary source:

- Freeman, S., Eddy, S. L., McDonough, M., Smith, M. K., Okoroafor,
  N., Jordt, H., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014). *Active learning increases
  student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111>

## 9. Self-regulated learning

Evidence: **Moderate as an organizing model**

Zimmerman describes self-regulation as a cyclical process involving forethought,
performance/monitoring, and self-reflection. This fits a learning coach that
must help a user plan, act, evaluate evidence, and adapt.

Product application:

- forethought: define outcome, strategy, schedule, and confidence;
- performance: focus attention, use strategies, and capture evidence;
- reflection: compare outcome to criteria and change the next plan;
- distinguish self-reported effort from observed performance.

Boundary:

- reflection without a changed action is journaling, not regulation;
- planning can become procrastination;
- weak domain knowledge limits the learner's ability to self-assess, so external
  rubrics and sources remain necessary.

Primary source:

- Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). *Becoming a self-regulated learner: An
  overview*. <https://doi.org/10.1207/S15430421TIP4102_2>

## 10. Deliberate practice

Evidence: **Conditional and domain-sensitive**

Deliberate practice means structured practice aimed at a weakness with feedback,
not merely repetition or total hours. Macnamara and colleagues found that its
relationship with performance varied considerably across games, music, sports,
education, and professions. It is important but not a universal explanation of
expertise.

Product application:

- use it for measurable subskills;
- isolate a weak component;
- choose a task just beyond reliable performance;
- provide rapid criterion-based feedback;
- repeat with variation and stop when quality deteriorates.

Boundary:

- not every knowledge domain has a stable task, expert rubric, or rapid
  feedback loop;
- practice volume alone is not deliberate practice;
- it cannot erase differences in opportunity, prior knowledge, or domain
  constraints.

Primary source:

- Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., & Oswald, F. L. (2014).
  *Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education,
  and professions: A meta-analysis*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810>

## 11. Knowledge tracing

Evidence: **Conditional product-model basis**

Corbett and Anderson modeled changing procedural knowledge from learner
opportunities and responses. Knowledge tracing is relevant to adaptation, but
its probability estimates depend on model assumptions and data quality.

Product application for v0.1:

- use a transparent evidence-state model rather than claiming precise
  probabilities;
- retain each attempt and transition;
- allow later Bayesian or other tracing models to consume the event log;
- never expose an opaque mastery probability as ground truth.

Primary source:

- Corbett, A. T., & Anderson, J. R. (1995). *Knowledge tracing:
  Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821>

## 12. Techniques Kongzi must not overclaim

### Learning styles

Pashler and colleagues found no adequate evidence base for matching instruction
to diagnosed visual/auditory/kinesthetic learning styles. Kongzi may honor
format preferences for accessibility or engagement, but must not claim that a
preferred sensory format is the user's optimal learning mechanism.

Source:

- Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008).
  *Learning styles: Concepts and evidence*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x>

### Highlighting and rereading

Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and distributed practice as high
utility across the conditions reviewed, while highlighting and rereading
received low-utility assessments. Kongzi may use highlights as navigation or
note anchors, but not as mastery evidence.

Source:

- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., &
  Willingham, D. T. (2013). *Improving students' learning with effective
  learning techniques*.
  <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266>

### Bloom's taxonomy

Bloom-style taxonomies can help vary task demands, but a taxonomy is not a study
method, a guaranteed sequence, or a mastery measure. Kongzi uses explicit
domain rubrics and evidence instead.

### Mind maps and notes

Maps help organize and navigate knowledge. They do not demonstrate recall or
application. Kongzi pairs them with questions and performance.

## 13. Personalization rules derived from the evidence

Kongzi personalizes on:

- prerequisite performance;
- knowledge type;
- target capability and retention horizon;
- error pattern;
- response latency and confidence calibration;
- number and spacing of successful retrievals;
- available time and missed-session history;
- accessibility and language needs;
- learner-selected context and motivation.

Kongzi does not personalize on:

- unsupported learning-style categories;
- personality stereotypes;
- the mentor persona's preferences;
- one self-reported “I understand” response;
- time spent or notes produced without performance evidence.

## 14. Product decision matrix

| Situation | Default response |
| --- | --- |
| New novice in structured domain | Worked example + self-explanation + faded problem |
| Fact-heavy material | Retrieval practice + spacing |
| Confusable categories | Compare/contrast + interleaving |
| Conceptual misconception | Explanation, counterexample, source check, re-explanation |
| Procedural error | Diagnose step, completion example, independent retry |
| Correct but low confidence | Delayed retrieval and calibration feedback |
| Confident wrong answer | Misconception record, corrective feedback, near-term retest |
| Real-world judgment | Case, criteria, prediction, outcome review |
| Motor/perceptual skill | External practice evidence; no chat-only mastery claim |
| Missed schedule | Minimum viable session and transparent re-plan |

## 15. Review policy

This evidence basis should be re-reviewed when:

- a product claim is challenged;
- a new core learning method is proposed;
- an existing method changes the mastery algorithm;
- a cited review is superseded by a credible newer synthesis;
- dogfood evidence shows repeated harm or a domain-specific failure.

Updates preserve the prior decision and state what changed.
